Aseptic Equipment Training — Industrial XR

Designing a user-centred immersive training experience for complex pharmaceutical manufacturing workflows.

XR Experience Design Overview

This case study demonstrates how complex, sequence-sensitive manufacturing training can be structured around human comprehension—translating dense procedural instructions into an intuitive user journey, focused spatial interface, and recoverable Unity-based XR experience.

Approached primarily from an XR Experience Designer perspective, the work centres on understanding trainee mental models, eliminating spatial UI overload, connecting instructions to physical equipment, and designing layered guidance that builds independent operator confidence.

02 · The Design Challenge

Why traditional training struggles in controlled manufacturing environments.

In controlled pharmaceutical manufacturing environments, operators must understand unfamiliar equipment, follow structured procedures, recognise critical components, and complete tasks in exact order. Relying solely on written manuals, slides, or brief shadowing leaves a wide gap between reading instructions and executing them calmly inside a real cleanroom.

Core Design Question

How might we make complex equipment training easier to understand, follow, and practise through an intuitive XR experience?

Six Human Learning Friction Points

  1. 01

    Understanding spatial equipment layout

    Two-dimensional diagrams flatten depth, making it hard for new operators to build a mental map of where controls, ports, and sterile zones sit relative to their body.

  2. 02

    Connecting instructions to physical parts

    Switching attention back and forth between written procedure sheets and physical equipment forces trainees to mentally translate abstract labels into real components.

  3. 03

    Remembering multi-step sequences

    Long procedural checklists overload working memory, especially when preparation steps, equipment actions, and verification checks blur together.

  4. 04

    Understanding error consequences

    Passive reading rarely communicates why step order matters or what goes wrong when a critical preparation or handling check is skipped.

  5. 05

    Recognising and recovering from mistakes

    Without immediate, low-stakes feedback, trainees hesitate when unsure—or struggle to understand how to correct an error without restarting from scratch.

  6. 06

    Building real-world confidence

    Limited physical access to live sterile environments restricts repetition, leaving first-time operators anxious when performing tasks under real production conditions.

03 · Understanding the Users

Designing around trainee mental models and supervisory goals.

To keep the experience grounded in real human needs rather than equipment mechanics, three distinct user profiles were defined to guide information hierarchy, pacing, and guidance depth.

Primary User · Profile

New Manufacturing Operator

Encountering aseptic preparation and barrier workstation workflows for the first time; needs clear orientation and low-stress repetition.

  • Understand the equipment and its components spatially.
  • Learn the task sequence without memory overload.
  • Know exactly what action to perform and where.
  • Receive clear confirmation after every interaction.
  • Understand how to recover from an incorrect action.
  • Practise safely without feeling overwhelmed.
Secondary User · Profile

Experienced Operator

Already familiar with routine operations; returns periodically to reinforce critical steps or confirm readiness without slow tutorials.

  • Refresh knowledge of the full workflow quickly.
  • Revisit specific stages on demand.
  • Practise high-attention actions efficiently.
  • Check independent understanding through assessment.
Stakeholder · Profile

Training Supervisor

Responsible for consistent onboarding quality and identifying where trainees hesitate before entering controlled production rooms.

  • Provide consistent, repeatable training experiences.
  • Observe the trainee’s progress clearly across stages.
  • Identify steps where additional guidance may be needed.

User Needs → Pain Points → Design Opportunities

User Group Core Need Observed Friction / Assumption Design Opportunity
New Operator Locate components and understand sequence Written labels do not map cleanly to unfamiliar hardware; dense screens cause cognitive fatigue. Pair one concise spatial instruction with a contextual object highlight on the active component.
New Operator Safe mistake recovery Uncertainty after an incorrect action breaks confidence and forces facilitator intervention. Deliver immediate, non-punitive error feedback explaining what happened and how to retry in place.
Experienced Operator Targeted refresher & self-check Locked linear tutorials feel slow; no separation between guided practice and independent recall. Provide on-demand step navigation alongside a proposed Assessment mode with reduced visual cues.

The profiles above represent structured user assumptions derived from industrial XR training challenges to guide design decisions prior to formal usability validation.

04 · Problem Definition & UX Audit

Identifying what breaks clarity inside immersive training interfaces.

Evaluating common usability patterns in industrial VR simulations surfaced seven recurring interface problems that distract trainees from the physical workflow. Addressing these systematically formed the foundation of the spatial UX architecture.

Audit Findings · Key Experience Breakdowns
  • Lack of clear hierarchy: Titles, phase labels, hints, and warnings compete at equal visual weight.
  • Disconnected instructions: Text panels sit far from the equipment part the trainee needs to inspect or manipulate.
  • Inconsistent panel placement: Shifting panel positions force constant head scanning and re-orientation between steps.
  • Small XR interaction targets: Desktop-sized buttons are tiring and error-prone to select in three-dimensional space.
Workflow & Feedback Breakdowns
  • Competing screen information: Persistent checklists, counters, and prompts crowd the user’s field of view simultaneously.
  • Limited error guidance: Incorrect selections either fail silently or display generic alerts without explaining how to recover.
  • Blurred learning and assessment: Keeping full prompts visible at all times prevents trainees from testing independent recall.

Problem-to-Opportunity Mapping

User Problem Design Opportunity Experience Impact
Too much information on screen Introduce progressive disclosure Keeps peripheral view open so the trainee focuses on the equipment first.
Unclear next action Use one focused instruction at a time Eliminates guesswork with concise, verb-first guidance for the active step.
Difficulty locating components Place contextual cues near relevant objects Bridges written instructions directly to physical workstation areas in 3D space.
Uncertainty after an error Provide immediate, actionable feedback Explains why an action failed and lets the user retry safely without restarting.
Difficulty revisiting instructions Make help and step navigation accessible on demand Supports self-paced review without cluttering the default view with permanent panels.
Inconsistent panel placement & small targets Standardise ergonomic viewing zones and target sizing Reduces neck fatigue and makes selection predictable inside a headset.
No distinction between learning & assessment Separate Learning Mode from proposed Assessment Mode Scaffolds beginners while letting confident operators verify unassisted recall.
05 · User Journey

Guiding a trainee from orientation to independent review.

The end-to-end training experience is structured into eight deliberate stages. Each stage balances what the trainee is trying to accomplish with how the spatial environment responds—progressively building familiarity before reducing guidance.

01

Introduction & Orientation

Orientation
User goal
Understand the training objective and feel comfortable navigating the XR space.
User action
Reviews brief welcome card and confirms basic selection controls.
System response
Presents calm entry view at comfortable eye height with one primary start action.
UX consideration
Avoid overwhelming first-time XR users with dense instructions before they settle into the scene.
02

Gowning Preparation

Preparation
User goal
Learn the correct sequence for cleanroom preparation items.
User action
Selects preparation items on the bench in the guided sequence.
System response
Highlights the next item, confirms valid order, and flags out-of-order picks gently.
UX consideration
Builds spatial selection muscle memory on simple objects before complex equipment tasks.
03

Entry Readiness

Preparation
User goal
Verify room-entry readiness checks before approaching the workstation.
User action
Reviews readiness indicators and confirms transition into the equipment area.
System response
Displays a clear visual confirmation checkpoint and opens the workstation zone.
UX consideration
Creates a crisp mental boundary between preparation and hands-on equipment work.
04

Equipment Familiarisation

Equipment Interaction
User goal
Recognise key areas and functional components of the aseptic workstation.
User action
Moves to the designated floor zone and inspects highlighted workstation components.
System response
Highlights zones one by one with concise contextual component labels.
UX consideration
Prevents trainees from hunting for parts during timed or sequence-critical steps.
05

Guided Training

Equipment Interaction
User goal
Follow a structured equipment task through clear sequential instructions.
User action
Reads the single active instruction, locates the highlighted part, and performs the step.
System response
Updates component state, displays brief confirmation, and advances smoothly.
UX consideration
One instruction and one active target keep cognitive load low across multi-step work.
06

Practice & Recovery

Equipment Interaction
User goal
Build confidence through repetition and learn safely from mistakes.
User action
Attempts steps independently; triggers Show Me or Retry if an error occurs.
System response
Replaces the active card with actionable error feedback and highlights the target to correct.
UX consideration
In-place recovery keeps trainees engaged instead of punishing mistakes with full restarts.
07

Assessment

Completion & Assessment
User goal
Demonstrate independent recall of the workflow with minimal prompting.
User action
Completes the task sequence without persistent object highlights or demonstrations.
System response
Tracks step accuracy quietly in the background (proposed assessment mode approach).
UX consideration
Separating assessment from learning avoids cue dependency while keeping controls identical.
08

Results & Review

Completion & Assessment
User goal
Understand overall performance and identify specific steps to revisit.
User action
Reviews summary card and chooses to revisit a stage or restart practice.
System response
Displays clear stage completion feedback and direct actions to practise weak steps.
UX consideration
Frames results constructively to support continuous learning rather than mere pass/fail grading.

Training Disclaimer: This journey illustrates a confidentiality-safe spatial UX structure using generic procedural stages. It does not reproduce proprietary pharmaceutical Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) and is not intended as operational manufacturing training material.

06 · Workflow & Information Architecture

Translating a multi-step procedure into predictable spatial structure.

Rather than presenting one long unbroken checklist, the experience groups tasks into four clear phases and pairs every step with consistent state communication—ensuring trainees always know where they are, what is expected, and how to recover if something goes wrong.

Phase 01

Orientation

Establishes training context, comfortable viewing distance, and core selection mechanics.

Phase 02

Preparation

Guides gowning order and room-entry readiness checks before approaching equipment.

Phase 03

Equipment Interaction

Connects step-by-step instructions to highlighted workstation parts with safe error recovery.

Phase 04

Completion & Assessment

Verifies unassisted recall (proposed mode) and presents actionable stage-level review.

Interaction Logic & Error-Recovery Flow Diagram

How the Interface Communicates State
  • Current phase: Displayed quietly at the top of the card so trainees retain macro orientation.
  • Current task & required action: One concise verb-first directive paired with a brief operational rationale.
  • Relevant equipment component: Signalled directly in 3D space through a clean component highlight cue.
  • Available assistance & completion status: Predictable footer controls (Show Me, Steps, Help) plus a continuous progress bar.
Why Information Architecture Matters in XR

In a physical training environment, a trainee can glance down at a printed clipboard. In XR, poorly structured UI competes directly with depth perception and equipment visibility. Anchoring every screen to a predictable five-question contract eliminates scanning overhead across every phase.

The Five Questions Answered on Every Step

01 Where am I?

Phase label and step progress bar anchor location in the overall procedure.

02 What do I need to do?

One focused, action-oriented instruction states the exact task.

03 Where do I interact?

A contextual highlight identifies the exact equipment component.

04 Was it successful?

Immediate visual confirmation validates success or explains an error.

05 What happens next?

Predictable Continue or Retry actions move the workflow forward cleanly.

07 · Spatial UI / UX Design

Adapting interface principles for three-dimensional space.

Conventional 2D layouts break down inside a headset when multiple panels crowd peripheral vision or sit outside comfortable viewing distances. The spatial UI system replaces multi-panel clutter with a single, calm, task-focused instructional card designed around human field of view and equipment sightlines.

  1. 01

    Clarity

    Display one primary instruction at a time using concise, action-oriented language and an unmistakable typographic hierarchy.

  2. 02

    Spatial Context

    Keep instructions inside the user’s natural field of view and near relevant equipment without blocking critical workstation sightlines.

  3. 03

    Interaction Comfort

    Use generous, appropriately sized interaction targets at comfortable viewing distances, minimising unnecessary head movement and keeping controls predictable.

  4. 04

    Progressive Disclosure

    Reveal only the information needed for the current task, keeping full step lists and help content tucked away until explicitly requested.

Main Spatial Interface Anatomy

Why One Focused Card Outperforms Multiple Panels

Simultaneous checklists, status bars, and help windows force operators to constantly shift focal depth and head angle inside a headset. Consolidating guidance into a single predictable spatial anchor keeps visual attention on the workstation while surfacing secondary controls strictly on demand.

08 · XR Interaction Design

Designing purposeful interactions beyond 3D visualisation.

Effective industrial XR training depends on clear cause-and-effect between trainee action and equipment response. Every interaction is designed around five core behaviours so users never wonder whether an object is interactive or whether their action registered.

  1. 01

    Object Identification

    Highlights the relevant workstation component, displays concise contextual information on selection, and clarifies how adjacent components relate.

  2. 02

    Guided Interaction

    Directs attention to the next relevant object, communicates the intended physical action clearly, and shows responsive visual feedback while interacting.

  3. 03

    Action Confirmation

    Validates completed actions immediately, explains unsuccessful or incomplete attempts clearly, and allows safe retry without losing place.

  4. 04

    Contextual Assistance

    Offers a Show Me visual demonstration, lets trainees revisit instructions freely, and makes help accessible on demand.

  5. 05

    Progress & Completion

    Communicates completed and pending tasks clearly, anchors the active stage, and concludes with a structured performance review.

09 · Guidance & Feedback System

Five layered cues that support learning without removing independence.

Too little guidance leaves beginners stuck; too much guidance turns training into mindless clicking. A five-layer architecture escalates assistance progressively—showing only what is helpful at the moment it is needed.

Five Conceptual Guidance Layers

Layer 01

Task Guidance

A concise, action-oriented instruction on the main spatial card describing the single task expected right now.

Layer 02

Object Guidance

A targeted visual highlight or positional cue anchored directly to the relevant component in the workstation.

Layer 03

Interaction Feedback

Immediate confirmation validating a completed step—or a clear, non-punitive recovery explanation after an incorrect selection.

Layer 04

Contextual Nudges

Optional gentle reminders triggered only if a trainee appears unsure or inactive, pointing attention toward the active zone without interrupting flow.

Layer 05

Demonstration (Show Me)

An on-demand visual demonstration illustrating the expected interaction clearly whenever text and highlights alone are not enough.

Two Complementary Experience Modes

Active Training Approach

Learning Mode

Designed for first-time operators building familiarity with equipment layout, sequence order, and safe handling habits.

  • Contextual object highlights enabled on every step.
  • Focused verb-first task instructions remain visible.
  • Show Me demonstrations and help are available on demand.
  • Errors explain clearly what happened and how to recover.
Proposed Design Approach

Assessment Mode

Proposed mode allowing experienced or newly trained operators to test independent recall without relying on visual scaffolding.

  • Reduced visual assistance with object cues switched off.
  • No unsolicited nudges or demonstrations during execution.
  • Trainee completes the workflow independently at their own pace.
  • Clear stage-by-stage results and improvement areas shown at the end.
10 · Figma — UX & UI Design Process

Evolving from a screen-based layout to a spatially considered XR interface.

Before building inside Unity, Figma was used to structure the content hierarchy, validate step relationships, test spatial ergonomics, and define reusable component tokens. Every visual decision below maps directly to a trainee comprehension or interaction challenge.

Wireframes & Flow Logic

Problem solved: Eliminates walls of text and dead-end screens by stripping each step down to one directive, one confirmation path, and one explicit recovery path.

Spatial Ergonomics & Sightlines

Problem solved: Prevents neck strain and hardware occlusion by placing cards within natural eye-line comfort while leaving the active work zone below fully unobstructed.

Reusable Tokens & States

Problem solved: Guarantees predictable visual behaviour and high contrast against bright cleanroom walls across default, focus, success, and error states.

Nine-Step Figma Design Workflow

Stage Design Activity User Need / Problem Addressed

02 Content hierarchy Prioritises phase context, primary verb-first instruction, and secondary help cleanly.
03 User flow & screen relationships Maps normal task progression alongside every error-recovery and help branch.
04 Wireframes Tests card compactness and reading speed before introducing visual styling.

11 · Unity — XR Prototype

Translating spatial UX decisions into an interactive Unity environment.

Unity serves as the implementation environment for this portfolio recreation—bringing the fictional cleanroom environment, world-space instructional panels, component highlights, and guided workflow logic together so spatial comfort and interaction pacing can be experienced directly in 3D space.

01 · Environment

Fictional Cleanroom & Generic Equipment

  • Calm, non-proprietary pharmaceutical training room with clear spatial zoning.
  • Generic modular barrier workstation and utility cart modelled for clear affordances.
  • Uncluttered visual backdrop that keeps focus squarely on interactive areas.
02 · Interface

World-Space Unity UI Panels

  • World-space instructional canvas positioned at comfortable eye height above the active zone.
  • Spatially positioned, generously sized buttons for dependable selection.
  • High-contrast dark translucent surface with consistent typographic hierarchy.
03 · Interaction

State-Driven Equipment Feedback

  • Direct object selection and contextual component highlighting on the active target.
  • Structured step progression tied cleanly to task state transitions.
  • Immediate success confirmation, actionable error feedback, and in-place retry/help actions.
04 · Experience Flow

Cohesive Training Arc

  • Introduction & orientation welcoming the trainee into the cleanroom space.
  • Guided equipment task with layered cues, followed by practice and error recovery.
  • Assessment concept (planned feature) concluding with a structured results review.

12 · Validation & Experience Evaluation

Evaluating clarity, comfort, and independent task confidence.

Formal usability testing has not yet been conducted for this portfolio recreation. Before moving from concept prototype toward real training deployment, the experience should be evaluated through structured formative sessions with both first-time XR users and experienced operators (subject to access and approval).

Proposed UX Evaluation Questions

  1. Q1Can a first-time user understand the interface without verbal coaching?
  2. Q2Can the user immediately identify the next required action?
  3. Q3Can the user locate the correct equipment component from spatial cues?
  4. Q4Are interaction targets and viewing distances comfortable to use throughout the task?
  5. Q5Does the user clearly understand success confirmations and error feedback?
  6. Q6Can the user recover from a mistake cleanly without restarting the entire workflow?
  7. Q7Can the user complete the task sequence confidently when assistance is reduced?

Transparency Summary — Decisions vs. Prototype Scope vs. Future Testing

Design Decisions Made
  • Structured 8-stage user journey across 4 clear phases.
  • Consolidated multi-panel clutter into one focused spatial card.
  • Defined 5-layer progressive guidance and in-place error recovery.
  • Separated Learning Mode from a proposed Assessment Mode.
Prototype Scope
  • Confidentiality-safe fictional environment and generic workstation.
  • Figma spatial UI system, tokens, and interaction states.
  • Unity world-space UI architecture and guided step-flow concept.

Proposed Future Testing
  • Moderated usability walkthroughs with first-time XR users.
  • Domain feedback sessions with experienced operators (pending access).
  • Ergonomic comfort and readability checks inside a physical headset.
  • Refinement of guidance timing and error copy based on observation.
13 · Outcome & Design Reflection

The design value of human-centred industrial XR.

Approaching industrial training as an XR Experience Design problem shifts the goal from simply visualising machinery in 3D to actively supporting how people perceive space, absorb multi-step sequences, and learn safely through action.

  1. 01

    Structured user journey

    Transforms an intimidating multi-step manufacturing workflow into eight logical, approachable stages.

  2. 02

    Reduced cognitive overload

    Replaces competing floating panels with one calm, focused spatial card and progressive disclosure.

  3. 03

    Instructions linked to physical space

    Connects concise directives directly to highlighted equipment components so trainees learn by doing.

  4. 04

    Predictable, recoverable interactions

    Turns mistakes into safe learning moments with immediate explanations and in-place retry actions.

  5. 05

    Support without dependency

    Layers guidance thoughtfully so beginners feel supported while confident operators can build independence.

  6. 06

    UX translated into Unity XR

    Carries information architecture, typography, and interaction rules cohesively from Figma into Unity.

Design Reflection
“Industrial XR is not simply about placing 3D content into a headset. It is about understanding the user’s environment, designing around real tasks, and making complex interactions easier to understand and perform.”
